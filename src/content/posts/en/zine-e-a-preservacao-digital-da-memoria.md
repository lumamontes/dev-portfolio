Since this month's [Clube de Zines](https://www.instagram.com/p/DdMFob5ICn-/?utm_source=ig_web_copy_link&stkn=MzRlODBiNWFlZA==) theme is **“Archive”** _(inspired, by the way, by my recent obsession with digital preservation)_, I came to write a little about something I find really interesting: _**zines as a way of preserving memory.**_

That's because the zine, by encouraging an authorial, creative approach with no fixed rules, becomes a very accessible and independent way to circulate ideas, and it's commonly associated with countercultural movements, marginalized groups and even fan communities. Anyone can make a zine ([even me!](https://www.biblioteca-de-zines.com.br/zines/luana-goes-gemulas)), and that opens up a huge range of possibilities for what gets made.

> #### In other words: zines give us what I can only describe as STROKES OF THE PEN from a specific place and time, portrayed in an original, one-of-a-kind way by whoever made that publication, along with the state of the world/society around them.

That can be anything from a moment in culture to the portrait of a community, a little piece of someone's life, or even the struggle of a social movement.

What I find interesting is that a zine doesn't need to have been made with the intention of becoming a grand historical record to preserve memory. Sometimes the person just wanted to make a zine, and years later we look at it and get to know a piece of a person's or community's life :)))

## Now: how do we make these publications survive the limbo of time in an accessible way?

Zines usually circulate in small print runs, often through trades, friends or fairs. Over time, copies can become hard to find. Physical spaces like zine libraries help look after these publications, but many records never make it into those spaces (or people never make it there).

This is where a really cool topic comes in: **community archives**. Put simply, they're initiatives where the community itself takes part in deciding how to gather, organize and care for its records, usually to preserve its own memory.

That can be independent work or done with a partner institution, but for independent community archives, technology opens up ways to make copies and spread this material: **building a digital collection on the internet, while also respecting how (and whether) zinesters want their publications to circulate.**

In the zine world, there are a few independent community archives with digital collections for keeping and finding these publications:

*   [QZAP — Queer Zine Archive Project](https://archive.qzap.org/)
    
*   [FANAC — An archive of science fiction fan publications and records](https://fanac.org/fanzines/)
    
*   [Sherwood Forest Zine Library — A zine library with a digital collection](https://www.sherwoodforestzinelibrary.org/)
    
*   [And, of course, the Biblioteca Brasileira de Zines itself!](https://www.biblioteca-de-zines.com.br/)
    

(We already have a post [here](https://bibliotecadezines.substack.com/p/como-digitalizar-seus-zines?r=53s7hh&utm_campaign=post&utm_medium=web) on how to **digitize your zines**, for anyone who wants to start sending theirs to us)

## So here was my [cuíra](https://www.dicio.com.br/cuira/) (restless itch) about this whole thing:

> `How can a community that's often independent and short-staffed keep this kind of digital archive going for a long time, respecting its own choices and its authors' rights, without depending on a single person and at the lowest possible cost?`

On the **technical side**, that means thinking about software, file storage and databases, metadata schemas for cataloguing, stable identifiers, backups in different places and other infrastructure, restoring, exporting and migrating data so you can switch systems in the future + documenting all of it for whoever contributes later. That can be complicated without specialized support, and there are many other factors involving technology.

But there's also the **organizational work** of agreeing on and coordinating responsibilities, curating and cataloguing materials, keeping a dialogue with zinesters about how their publications show up in the collection, how to carry on through team changes, and more.

Bringing it back to Biblioteca de Zines, this is something we're actively discussing for the long-term structure of our collection (_you can join the discussion on [github](https://github.com/lumamontes/biblioteca-de-zines/discussions), in the comments or on other social media_)! **We want to do our homework, learning from existing initiatives and working out how best to care for the collection and for the people who share their zines with us.**

### Luckily, there are lots of free software initiatives, plus guidance materials on this subject.

I'll leave some resources linked below:

*   [Tainacan](https://tainacan.org/en/) — free software for organizing and publishing digital collections
    
*   [Omeka](https://omeka.org/) — open source software for digital collections and exhibits
    
*   [Archivematica](https://www.archivematica.org/en/) — free software for digital preservation
    
*   [NDSA](https://www.ndsa.org/publications/levels-of-digital-preservation/) — levels of digital preservation
    
*   [Zine Librarians Code of Ethics](https://www.zinelibraries.info/code-of-ethics/) — caring for zines, collections and the people who make them
    

For the library and the reality of zines, some tools need to be adapted, and some solutions may need to be built as open source. **What we learn and build along the way can help other people too.**

And since I can't keep a secret, we also have some ideas for extending this conversation and sharing our technology and learnings with other communities or people who want to build their own digital collections _(coming soon)_
